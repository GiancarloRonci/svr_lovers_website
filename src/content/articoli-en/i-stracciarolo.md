## Meaning of the Poem

“I Stracciarolo” is the travelling rag-and-bone man who once used to come through San Vito. He arrived loaded beyond belief, with “bagnarole” (tin tubs) and buckets that he handed out in exchange for old things. His cry, “Ladies, the stracciarolo is coming!”, could be heard all the way down at the Canale and at the Moro; up on the Monte of San Vito it echoed back, and in [Piazza San Biagio](/en/luoghi/piazza-san-biagio/) people gathered round.

At first the author speaks well of him: he was a decent man, he would take your leaky basin and give you a tub, and if he spotted something else half dented he would throw in a bucket and call it a day. He had a tenor's voice and slipped into the little alleys and under the doorways; he often knocked on the window panes: “bring me the leaky pot you cook your soup in”.

But many times the stracciarolo forgot his manners. He would come into the house and carry off even the side of the bed; the folding bed, if it was just a little rusty, he grabbed and loaded onto his back. He took the chamber pots people had in those days too, and only after thinking it over did he leave a tub. Once he came into a house and took everything: he unhooked the sausages from the beam and walked off with the ham, leaving a colander in return; and if there was rolled pancetta, he did not even leave the paper.

Scrap iron was the last thing on his mind: he took all the wine in the cask and emptied the “vettina”, the earthenware jar, as well, since he had some twenty buckets anyway. Just remembering it makes the author's blood boil: from his mother he took a jar of vinegar, all decorated with Egyptian designs. Running after him was no use: he was no fool, and threw away tubs and buckets to get away faster. Who knows what that beautiful jar was worth, but it broke while he was running, and below Santa Maria the stink of vinegar was so strong you could hardly walk. That was the stracciarolo who came through San Vito, and the poem ends with a playful curse against him.
