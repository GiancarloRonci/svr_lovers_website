*When progress takes us all in the same direction, perhaps it is time to change course.*

## From the exodus from the villages to the return to the land

There is a question we ought to ask ourselves: are we really sure that the model of development that led us from small villages to big cities has, in every respect, produced a better quality of life? Perhaps?

For decades we followed a tune: the tune of work, of opportunities, of services and of progress.

As in the tale of the Pied Piper of Hamelin, entire generations followed that melody, leaving towns and countryside behind to reach the big cities.
Not on a whim, but in search of a better future.

Meanwhile the villages were losing inhabitants, businesses, services, relationships and identity. The metropolises grew, piling up traffic, congestion, land consumption and ever harder everyday lives.

Today we must ask ourselves whether a city can grow so large that it offers more and more opportunities on paper while, at the same time, shrinking people's room for freedom, individuality and relationships. Our friend from the Piazza, Maurizio, knows this well: like others before him, he has chosen to live in San Vito Romano too.

What if the time has come to change the tune?

There is another story that helps us reflect: *The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas*, by Ursula K. Le Guin.
A community lives happily, but its happiness depends on the hidden suffering of a child.

A terrible metaphor: can we consider ourselves a truly happy society if the well-being of some coexists with the abandonment of others?

Let us think of the small towns, of the young people forced to leave, of the elderly left behind in villages where services and chances to meet become ever harder to find.

This is not a contest between cities and villages. It is a reflection on the imbalances produced by a model that has gradually concentrated opportunities and resources.

## Counter-urbanisation: not going back, but changing direction

We should not long for a past that cannot return exactly as it was.
We must make the places we left attractive again.

Work, services, transport links, technology, the restoration of old buildings, sustainable tourism and new economic activities can all help build an alternative.

But an overall vision is needed.
Renovating a few buildings or opening a public work is not enough.
We need to rebuild a system in which people can choose to live, work, meet and plan their future.

And beware of the Leopard!

> "If we want everything to stay as it is, everything must change."

This is the risk of a regeneration that is only skin-deep: changing the façades without changing living conditions, building works without creating new opportunities.

To regenerate means to act on the causes, not only on the effects.

## A new melody

We do not need to convince everyone to head for the same place in search of a better life.
We need to make sure that more places can offer a dignified life, opportunities and genuine relationships.

Because a community is not made only of buildings, streets and services. It is made of people who recognise one another, take part and feel they are the protagonists of the place where they live.

Progress should not ask anyone to sacrifice themselves so that someone else can be better off.

So, coming back to our villages, let us ask ourselves a question: what if the future were no longer just about moving towards the big cities, but about giving the places we come from a reason to stay, to return and to start again?

We do not want to turn back the hands of the clock.
We want to set going again the ones that have stopped.

A village must not become a museum of its own past. It must be able to be the place where someone chooses to build their future.

An open reflection. Because change, before it becomes a project, must become a shared question. Ideas should spark discussion, not division.
