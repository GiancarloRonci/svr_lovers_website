Anyone who has spent an afternoon in a library knows that books alone are not enough. You need tidy shelves, labels on the spines, cards listing author, title and subject, and above all you need someone to ask: the librarian, who knows where a volume is, whether it is up to date, whether a better edition exists. Without all this, even the richest library becomes a paper warehouse that you walk into with a question and leave with a headache.

Exactly the same thing happens with data. Today every organisation, from a large company to a town council, from an association to a small shop, produces and stores a huge amount of information: spreadsheets, databases, reports, indicators, tables exported from some management system. The problem is no longer having data, but finding it, understanding it and using it with confidence. This is where the **data catalog** comes in: in short, the library index for your data.

## A simple definition

A data catalog is a **central, searchable directory** that lists the available data (the so-called *data assets*), explains what it is and helps people **find and use the right data with confidence**.

It is not a new database and it does not replace the systems where the data is actually stored. Rather, it is a layer that sits on top of them and gathers information *about* the data, the so-called metadata: what a piece of data is called, what it measures, how it is calculated, which system it comes from, when it was last updated, who is responsible for it and how reliable it is. Just as a library card does not contain the book, but tells you everything you need to find it and decide whether it is the right one.

The ultimate goal can be summed up in three words: **find, understand, use**. And, as a result, three values every organisation looks for in its data: **quality**, **trust** and **value**.

## The library analogy

To really understand what a catalog is for, it helps to imagine two scenarios.

### Without a catalog

Imagine walking into a library with no card index, no signs and no staff. The books are there, thousands of them, but they are piled on tables and shelves in no obvious order. What happens?

- **You don't know which books exist.** Maybe the volume you want is there, maybe not. The only way to find out is to dig through the piles and hope for the best.
- **The same title means different things to different people.** Two books called "History of the Town" may be about different towns or different periods, and nobody tells you.
- **You rely on word of mouth.** "Ask Mario, he found it last time." But Mario isn't here today, and he may not remember correctly.
- **You risk using the wrong or an outdated book.** You grab the first volume that looks right, without knowing that a newer, corrected edition exists.

In the world of data these problems are an everyday occurrence. A colleague asks "how many customers do we have?" and gets three different answers from three different departments, because each counts differently: one includes inactive customers, one doesn't, one counts households and another individuals. A report is built on a table nobody has updated for two years. A newcomer spends weeks just figuring out where the information they need lives. Lost time, decisions made on shaky ground, endless arguments over numbers that don't add up.

### With a data catalog

Now imagine the same library, but with a well-made catalog: a terminal with a search bar, clear entries, a librarian on hand. You search for "sales", "customers" or "recycling rate" and immediately find the right entries, each with a tick showing its status. Now you can:

- **Find what data exists**: indicators, datasets, reports, tables, all listed in one place.
- **Understand it**: for each entry there is the definition, the formula and the rules it is built on.
- **Know who to ask**: every piece of data has someone responsible for it, the *data owner* or *data steward*, the "librarian" for that piece of knowledge.
- **Trust it**: the catalog shows the status of the data, its quality and guidance on how to use it. You know whether it is certified, whether to use it with caution, or whether it is better left alone.

The difference between the two scenarios is not in the data, which is the same. It is in being able to find your way around.

## What you get in the catalog

A good data catalog organises the information about each piece of data into a few recurring sections. Each one has its counterpart in the world of the library.

### 1. Data assets

The list of available data, like a library's collection arranged from A to Z. Here you find database tables, files, reports, dashboards and indicators. Simply knowing that a certain piece of data *exists* is already a huge step forward: how often do people rebuild from scratch something someone else had already calculated, perhaps better?

### 2. Definition

A clear description of what the data means: the "summary" of the book, the blurb on the back cover. What do we mean by an "active customer"? What counts as "attendance" at an event? What does "resident" mean in a council statistic? The definition is where an organisation decides to speak one language. It may seem a detail, but it is often the most valuable part of the catalog, because most disagreements about numbers come from different definitions, not wrong calculations.

### 3. Formula and rules

How the data is calculated and which business rules govern it: the "content and method" of the book. If the recycling rate is the ratio of separately collected waste to total waste, which waste counts towards the total? Do bulky items count? What about home composting? Writing the formula down in black and white lets anyone check the result, reproduce it and understand why two apparently similar numbers don't match.

### 4. Source and lineage

Where the data comes from and how it was built: the book's "origin and journey". In the jargon this is called *lineage*, the data's family tree. An indicator that appears in a report is often the result of a long chain of steps: a form filled in, a system that records it, a nightly extraction, an intermediate table, a final calculation. Knowing this chain serves two purposes. The first is trust: I know where the number comes from and can judge how solid it is. The second is maintenance: if something changes at the start of the chain, I know straight away which reports and indicators will be affected.

### 5. Owner and steward

The person to contact with questions: the "librarian in charge" of that shelf. The *data owner* is responsible for the data from a business point of view, meaning they decide what it means and how it should be used; the *data steward* looks after it day to day, checks its quality and answers questions. Having a name next to every piece of data changes everything: instead of going from office to office asking "does anyone know about this table?", you know exactly who to write to.

### 6. Status and quality

The data's level of validation, a sort of seal. The most common labels are three: **trusted** (the data is certified and can be used freely), **use with caution** (the data is useful but has known limits, for example it is incomplete or rarely updated), **do not use** (the data is obsolete, replaced or wrong, and stays in the catalog only so that nobody uses it by mistake). This section is what turns the catalog from a simple list into a tool of trust.

## The benefits

Why should an organisation invest time in building and maintaining a catalog? The advantages can be summed up in three big chapters.

### Save time

Find the right data quickly. Studies of analysts' work have shown for years that a significant part of their day goes not on actual analysis but on searching for and checking data. A catalog cuts this time drastically: searching becomes a matter of seconds, and getting a newcomer up to speed shrinks from weeks to days. It also avoids duplicates, the same indicator calculated three times by three different people with three slightly different results.

### Speak the same language

Shared, clear and consistent definitions across teams. When sales and accounts use the same definition of "customer", meetings stop turning into arguments about which number is right and go back to what matters: what to do. The catalog becomes a kind of common vocabulary, the organisation's official glossary, which everyone can refer to.

### Make better decisions

Use trusted, well-understood data with confidence. A decision is only as good as the information it is based on. Knowing that an indicator is certified, knowing its formula and origin, having someone responsible to ask for clarification: all this reduces the risk of choosing on the basis of a wrong or misunderstood number. And when someone asks "where does this figure come from?", the answer is ready.

## An example close to home

Let's bring this down to a reality we know well: a small municipality like San Vito Romano. Even a council serving a few thousand people handles a great deal of data: the population register, local taxes, waste collection, building permits, tourist numbers, events, schools, social services.

Take an indicator that often appears in local news: the **recycling rate**. In a catalog of council data, its entry might contain:

- **Asset**: "Annual percentage of separate waste collection".
- **Definition**: the share of municipal waste collected separately out of the total municipal waste produced in the municipality in a year.
- **Formula and rules**: separately collected waste divided by total waste, times one hundred, according to the calculation criteria set at national and regional level, stating which fractions are included.
- **Source**: the weighing data reported by the waste service operator, then passed on to the bodies that certify it.
- **Owner**: the council office that oversees the waste service.
- **Status**: trusted for years already certified, use with caution for the current year, which is still provisional.

With an entry like this, a resident, a councillor or a local journalist knows exactly what they are looking at. There are no more arguments about "the real number", because the entry says where it comes from, how it is calculated and how final it is.

When you think about it, a website like ours works a bit like a catalog too: it gathers places, walks, events, stories and news about San Vito Romano, describes them, sorts them into categories and makes them easy to find. Without an index, all this information would stay scattered across memories, social media groups and word of mouth. With an index, it becomes a heritage anyone can consult.

## How to build a catalog

There is no need to start with a huge project. The best experiences usually follow a few simple steps.

1. **Start with the most used data.** Instead of recording everything, begin with the ten or twenty indicators that turn up most often in reports and meetings. They are the ones that raise the most questions and the most arguments.
2. **Write the definitions together.** Definitions are not handed down from above: they are agreed between those who produce the data and those who use it. It takes a few meetings, but it pays off enormously.
3. **Name an owner for everything.** Every piece of data in the catalog needs an owner and, if possible, a steward. Data without an owner is data that sooner or later nobody will update.
4. **Be honest about status.** It is better to state that data should be "used with caution" than to let people believe it is perfect. Trust comes from transparency, not perfection.
5. **Keep it alive.** A catalog is not a document you write once and file away. Data changes, new indicators appear, others become obsolete. It needs a little regular upkeep, just as a library adds new acquisitions and removes damaged volumes.

There are dedicated software tools, some very sophisticated, able to read databases automatically and rebuild the data's lineage. But to get started, a shared spreadsheet or a well-organised wiki page can be enough. The tool matters less than the habit: describing the data, giving it an owner and stating its quality.

## Mistakes to avoid

Catalogs can fail too. The most frequent cases are three. The first is the **graveyard catalog**: built with enthusiasm, never updated, full of entries describing data that has long since disappeared. The second is the **encyclopaedia catalog**: it tries to describe everything, down to the last column of the last table, and the work never ends. The third is the **ownerless catalog**: lots of entries, no names, nobody to ask. In all three cases the problem is organisational, not technical, and the solution is the same: start small, assign clear responsibilities and treat the catalog as a service, not a project with an end date.

## In short

A data catalog is to data what the card index and the librarian are to books. It does not create new information, but makes the information that already exists usable. It tells you **what data there is**, **what it means**, **how it is calculated**, **where it comes from**, **who to ask** and **how far you can trust it**.

The results show on three fronts: you **save time**, because the right data can be found in seconds; you **speak the same language**, because definitions are shared; you **make better decisions**, because you rely on trusted, well-understood data.

In a world where data grows every day, true wealth is not owning a lot of it, but knowing where it is and which of it to trust. Just as in a good library, where the value lies not only in the number of volumes, but in being able to find the right book in a few minutes.
