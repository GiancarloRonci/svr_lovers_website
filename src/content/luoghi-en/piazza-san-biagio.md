Piazza San Biagio is one of the hearts of San Vito Romano's old town: a small square paved in cobblestones, overlooked by the parish church of San Biagio Vescovo e Martire, which stands at the centre of the network of alleys and stairways that make up the town's medieval core. The church's construction date is uncertain: tradition holds that it was built on the site of an earlier 12th-century oratory. The present building is the result of the rebuilding promoted by the Theodoli family between 1607 and 1609, to a design by the architect Giovanni Theodoli. The square itself was redeveloped in 1646, and the church was formally consecrated on 13 October 1777.

The Baroque interior houses, above the high altar, an early 17th-century fresco (possibly from 1609) by an unknown late-Mannerist artist of the Roman school, depicting Saint Blaise saving a child from a fishbone stuck in his throat, together with a woman. This is the miracle behind the saint's cult as protector of the throat: every year, on 3 February, the tradition of the blessing of the throats is renewed.

In the 19th century the two chapels of the Rosary and of Saint Joseph were added on either side of the presbytery, in 1831 and 1847 respectively. The first restorations, in the early 20th century, heavily altered the original quality of the frescoes: of the old painted decoration, some frescoes by an unknown artist with various figures of saints survive in the side chapels, completed and retouched by Antonelli around 1928. Between 1927 and 1929 the interior was also enriched with marble altars and new flooring. Also worth noting is the 17th-century baptismal font, made up of a porphyry basin topped by an opening tabernacle in painted and gilded walnut.

By day the square is a quiet crossing point among the alleys of the old town; in the evening, with the streetlamps lighting up the stone façades, it becomes one of San Vito Romano's most evocative corners, especially during festivals, when it fills with colourful bunting.

## A Baroque gem reawakened: the church in the 18th century

*Notes by Agapito Fornari.*

At the beginning of the 18th century, the Church of San Biagio still kept the structure and size it had had in the previous century, but it needed maintenance work. The records of the pastoral visits paint the picture of a building that, over the course of the century, would undergo works, repairs and new furnishings.

### The 1703 visit: light, upkeep and everyday life

The pastoral visit of 1703 highlighted several problems. The walls needed whitewashing and the interior was poorly lit: the building of the new organ had in fact reduced the amount of light coming in. Orders were therefore given to enlarge the east window, above the high altar, with a cloth to screen it during Lent.

An everyday need also found its place among the instructions: the faithful complained that they could not hear the chimes of the other parish's clock. The church was therefore asked to have a clock of its own, to mark the hours for the people living in the area.

Other works concerned the spaces and the sacred images. A protective railing was prescribed for the baptismal font, while in the chapel of Saint Francis the image of the saint was so deteriorated that it needed restoring or remaking entirely.

### The bell tower and Girolamo Theodoli's intervention

One of the most urgent issues was the bell tower. In 1703 an order dating back some forty years had still not been carried out, while rainwater leaks kept damaging the structure.

The administrators of the community, which claimed the credit for having built that "fine temple of elegant structure", were ordered to complete the works within a year, on pain of being banned from entering the church. If this was not done, the archpriest was to seek the support of the faithful and of Marquis Girolamo Theodoli.

The new bell tower was completed in 1715. Marquis Theodoli contributed to it as the author of the design and as a financial backer of the work, together with other benefactors and the income of the local confraternities. The tower held three bells, two of which were cast in 1702 and 1711 respectively.

### The furnishings and the care of the chapels

The Marquis's involvement also extended to the interior of the church. To his design, an oval walnut balustrade with three small gates was made, carved by Master Filippo Ronci, to enclose the high altar. Alongside it stood the new choir, also in walnut.

Care for the building continued in the following decades. In 1754 cleaning was prescribed for the paintings in the chapels of Saints Peter and Paul and of Saint Bartholomew: further evidence of the attention paid to preserving the sacred images.

### The consecration of 1777

An important milestone in the history of the church was its solemn consecration, celebrated by Monsignor Pietro Ruggeri, Bishop of Ruvo, in honour of the Mother of God and of Saint Blaise, bishop and martyr, co-patron of the town.

The official page of the Diocese of Tivoli and Palestrina gives the date as **13 October 1777**. That date is adopted here, noting the difference from the 12 October given in the source text.

From the maintenance requests of 1703 to the new furnishings and the consecration, the events of the 18th century tell the story of the relationship between the church and its community: a place of worship whose care involved administrators, confraternities, benefactors and craftsmen.

### Sources and archival research

The information on the church's 18th-century history is taken from the *Relazione Storica* (Historical Report) attached to the 2017 restoration project.

The report was coordinated and signed by the project architect Antonella Cefaro and the engineer Alessandra Spelta. Dr Cinzia Di Fazio, then director of the Diocesan Archive, helped identify the historical content and archival sources, working with the archivist Piero Scatizzi, who carried out the research in the field.

For Monsignor Pietro Ruggeri's title and for the date of the consecration, reference was also made to the church's page on the official website of the Diocese of Tivoli and Palestrina.
