In Tivoli, about an hour's drive from San Vito Romano, stands Villa d'Este, one of the most celebrated Renaissance gardens in the world and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2001. It was Cardinal Ippolito II d'Este, son of Lucrezia Borgia, who commissioned it in the mid-16th century, transforming a former Benedictine convent on the hillside of Tivoli into a lavish residence worthy of his rank.

The project, entrusted to the architect and archaeologist Pirro Ligorio, makes clever use of the site's steep slope to create a system of terraces linked by staircases, nymphaea and pathways, all animated by an ingenious hydraulic network fed by the waters of the Aniene river. The result is a sequence of monumental fountains: the Oval Fountain, the Neptune Fountain with its spectacular cascade, the Avenue of a Hundred Fountains, and the Water Organ Fountain, which once produced actual melodies through a compressed-air hydraulic mechanism, a true marvel of Renaissance engineering.

Conceived as a symbolic journey through mythology, allegory and references to classical antiquity, the garden profoundly influenced the art of garden design across Europe in the centuries that followed, inspiring the gardens of Versailles and many other royal residences. The villa's interiors, frescoed by artists such as Livio Agresti and Federico Zuccari, house decorative cycles dedicated to the labors of Hercules and the history of the Este family.

Today Villa d'Este is a state museum open to visitors year-round, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors each year who are captivated by the dialogue between architecture, nature and water that has made it a timeless model of garden art.
